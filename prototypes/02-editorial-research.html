Essay I. Teaching a language model to read a schedule

LLMs for Optimization: job-shop scheduling (JSSP). York University, Sep–Dec 2025.

A job-shop scheduling problem gives you jobs, each a fixed sequence of operations, and machines that can process one operation at a time. The goal is a schedule that finishes everything as early as possible. Solvers are good at this. The research question was whether a language model can learn to reason about it when the problem is written out as text.3.Code: github.com/PATELOM925/LLMs-for-Optimization-Problems. The 3×3 instance below is a toy example for exposition, not from the dataset.

Pipeline: JSSP instances were converted to disjunctive graphs (PyTorch Geometric), then serialized as constraints in prompts. About 130k raw instances were filtered to 9,525 usable ones. Fine-tuning runtime fell from 70h to 11.5h with 4-bit LoRA (Unsloth) on an RTX A6000.

Step 1. A raw instance

Each job lists its operations in order, as (machine, processing time). Order within a job is fixed; which job goes first on a shared machine is the decision.

Toy 3 jobs by 3 machines instance
JobOp 1Op 2Op 3
J1M1 · 3M2 · 2M3 · 2
J2M1 · 2M3 · 1M2 · 4
J3M2 · 4M3 · 3M1 · 1

Toy instance for exposition. Real instances were larger and came from a raw pool of roughly 130k.

Step 2. The disjunctive graph

Each operation becomes a node. Precedence edges (directed) encode order within a job. Machine-conflict edges (undirected, dashed) join operations that compete for the same machine; scheduling means choosing a direction for every one of them. In the project these graphs were built with PyTorch Geometric.

Hover or focus a node (Tab) to see its precedence and machine-conflict edges.

Step 3. Serialized into a prompt

The graph is written out as text: precedence as ordered chains and machine conflicts as groups that must not overlap. The model is asked for start times.

Problem: job-shop scheduling, 3 jobs x 3 machines.
Minimize makespan. Each machine runs one operation at a time.

Precedence (per job, in order):
  J1: O(1,1)[M1,3] -> O(1,2)[M2,2] -> O(1,3)[M3,2]
  J2: O(2,1)[M1,2] -> O(2,2)[M3,1] -> O(2,3)[M2,4]
  J3: O(3,1)[M2,4] -> O(3,2)[M3,3] -> O(3,3)[M1,1]

Machine conflicts (no overlap):
  M1: {O(1,1), O(2,1), O(3,3)}
  M2: {O(1,2), O(2,3), O(3,1)}
  M3: {O(1,3), O(2,2), O(3,2)}

Answer: a start time for every operation.

Illustrative format written for this page. The exact serialization is in the repository.

Step 4. Fine-tuning with 4-bit LoRA

The base model’s weights stay frozen and quantized to 4 bits; only a small low-rank update is trained. With Unsloth on a single RTX A6000, this made fine-tuning practical to iterate on.

The larger lesson was about data rather than models: most of the raw pool was not usable, and the filtering step shaped what the model could learn more than any hyperparameter did.4.The ratio is derived from the two published numbers: 9,525 usable out of roughly 130k raw, about 7%.

Essay II. Where a Gujarati sentiment model goes wrong

Low-resource Gujarati sentiment analysis. Research assistant with Prof. Uyen T. Nguyen, York University, Mar 2026 to present.

Gujarati has little labelled sentiment data. The project combines SST-2 translated into Gujarati with GSAC, a native Gujarati dataset, and compares TF-IDF baselines against MuRIL, a multilingual model for Indian languages. The aggregate score is only the start; the interesting part is the error analysis.5.This work is ongoing, so no results are reported here. The figure shows kinds of error, not their frequency.
